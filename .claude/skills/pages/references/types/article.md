<page_type name="article" mode="retrieval, then persuasion" goals="be read, then act" nav="full">

<job>
A blog post answers one question a real customer has, shows the company has done the thing, and
hands interested readers to the matching service page. Success is the reader getting the answer;
the second job is the path to the money page. A post earns its place by
answering a question people search for; thin or machine-made posts weigh on the whole site.
</job>

<reader>
Who searched for this question, in their words, and which service page they need next.
</reader>

<exists>
Decide first whether the post, or the blog, should exist. Keep a post only when `public/` and `brand/`
hold first-hand material for it: a job the company did, a question customers really ask, a local
fact, the owner's own view. Otherwise: improve a post that has traffic or links; merge overlapping
weak posts into one and redirect the rest; retire posts with no traffic, links or first-hand
material. Retiring a whole blog is legitimate when nothing survives. Mass-produced AI posts are
named spam. The case for retiring is quality and honesty, not a traffic promise.
</exists>

<inventory>
  answer      the direct answer, from the company's own text or experience
  experience  the job, photographs, numbers that prove they have done it
  author      a real, named person and the facts the brand holds about them
  service     the service page this topic leads to
  related     other posts that genuinely continue the reader's question
</inventory>

<select>
  - The answer first, then the detail: readers scan, and a summary at the top lets them decide.
  - First-hand experience beats general advice; a named job beats a claim of expertise.
  - Objective beats promotional: promotional language costs readability.
  - A date is changed only when the content substantially changes.
</select>

<sections>
In page order. `core` is on nearly every page of this type; everything else is a choice made
against the material. Each line says what the band holds, then when it earns its place.

  title       core      The question it answers, in the searcher's words.
  byline      core      A real, named author and labelled dates (published, updated).
  answer      core      The answer itself, first, in two or three sentences.
  body        core      Sections headed by what each covers; one idea per paragraph.
  evidence    optional  The job, photographs, numbers or a diagram that show the company has done it.
                        When: `public/` and `brand/` hold them. This is what makes the post worth having.
  contents    optional  A list of the sections, linked. When: the post is long enough to need it.
  sources     optional  Where outside facts came from, linked. When: the post cites any.
  service     core      An in-body link to the service, where the topic meets the offer.
  action      core      A closing call to action matched to the topic.
  author      optional  A short bio of the author, linking to the about page.
                        When: the author's qualifications are part of why to believe the post.
                        Core for health, money, legal and safety topics.
  related     optional  Directly below, only genuinely related posts; with none, the service action.
                        When: other posts really continue this reader's question.
</sections>

<phrase>
  - Headings name what the section is about, plainly, not cleverly.
  - Excerpts and intros state the finding, not a teaser.
  - Link text names the destination ("roof replacement"), never "click here".
  - No date in a title unless the content is truly time-bound.
  - Everything in this file describes a shape. Never reuse its wording, or another page's.
</phrase>

<show>
  - The company's own job photographs, a diagram of the actual problem, the real author. No image
    at all beats a stock hero. One real image serves sharing and article markup.
  - The constraint is measure, not word count: comfortable line length, generous line spacing,
    space between paragraphs.
</show>

<links>
Post → service page in the body, with the service named in the link. Service page → the posts that
answer its customers' questions. Related posts directly under the article, chosen by hand, labelled
by what they are. Series: previous and next, and the whole series listed. Breadcrumbs only where
the structure really is a hierarchy.
</links>

<variant name="index" label="blog index and category archive" mode="retrieval">
The index lets a reader find the one post that answers their question. A heading that says what the
blog covers and for whom; categories when there are several; entries newest first, with a few
evergreen posts on the main services pinned if useful. Each entry: title, a one-line summary of the
finding, date, topic, in the same structure every time; a thumbnail only where a real image exists,
never a placeholder. One page is best for a small blog; pagination means the archive needs
pruning. Categories mirror services and open with a line and a link to that service's page; a
category exists only when it has several posts; no tags on a small site.
</variant>

<industry>
Health, money, legal and safety topics need a qualified, named author, or the post should not
exist. For trades and local businesses the proof is the job: here is what we did, and the photograph.
</industry>

</page_type>
