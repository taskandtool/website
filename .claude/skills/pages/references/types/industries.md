<model>
Industries do not differ in design principles; they differ in the doubt the visitor arrives with,
and so in the proof that answers it. Answer five questions
once per site, before planning pages:

  1. What does the visitor doubt, in their words? Is it trust (are you real, good, safe) or
     logistics (are you open, what does it cost, can I book)? Returning customers and diners
     usually need logistics.
  2. What kind of thing is bought?
       a place        (restaurant, hotel)          photographs of every space are the product
       an interface   (software, app)              the real product, never illustration
       a document     (research, publishing)       the artifacts are the proof
       a service      (trades, cleaning)           past work and credentials; stock harms
       a person       (law, clinic, therapy)       named practitioners, credentials, clarity
                                                   about what they handle, fees
       goods          (retail)                     product images, delivery and returns
       a cause        (nonprofit)                  where the money goes, and what it did
  3. Decision cost × reversibility → how much proof, how long the page.
  4. Retrieval or persuasion → the shape of the text.
  5. What does the law require, or forbid, on this page in this jurisdiction? Required elements
     are correctness, not convention. The brand folder needs its jurisdiction.

Two more that hold everywhere:
  - Proof is increasingly checked off the site: reviews are read across several platforms and in
    AI summaries, and on-site testimonials are discounted by Google and regulated. Point to proof a
    reader can verify (platform, count, recency, a link), and keep the site's facts consistent with
    the Google profile.
  - Some businesses serve several audiences with different doubts (donor, volunteer, beneficiary;
    new and existing patient). The doubt is per audience, and the homepage routes between them.
</model>

<industry name="restaurant, café, bar">
Doubt: is it open, what's on, what does it cost, can I get a table (logistics). Proof: the menu
with prices, real photographs of dishes and room. Weight: menu, booking or ordering, hours and
location. Must-haves: hours, address, tap-to-call phone, booking, prices. Mistakes: PDF menus,
hours that contradict Google, hard-to-find booking.
</industry>

<industry name="hospitality">
Doubt: is it really like the photographs, and what is the real total. Proof: photographs of every
space, candid over staged; rating with count on the platforms travellers use; a clear cancellation
policy. Law: in the US the total price including mandatory fees must be shown up front for lodging
(from May 2025). Mistakes: fees added late, under-photographed rooms, no way to book direct.
</industry>

<industry name="home services and trades">
Doubt: are you legitimate, will you turn up, do you work where I live. Proof: licence number (some
states require it in advertising), named insurance, trade or manufacturer certifications, review
count and recency, real job photographs. Weight: one page per service, service area, reviews,
contact. Must-haves: places served by name, phone, emergency hours if offered; a business run from
home lists areas, not its address. Mistakes: "licensed and insured" with no number, stock workers.
</industry>

<industry name="professional services">
Doubt: do you handle problems like mine, and what will it cost. Buyers rule firms out most often
because they could not understand how the firm could help. Proof: clarity about what they handle,
named people with credentials, representative matters, reviews, fee structure. Law: results claims
must not create unjustified expectations; testimonials must disclose connections; UK solicitors
must publish prices for listed services, their SRA number and badge, and complaints procedure.
Weight: one page per practice area, people pages, fees.
</industry>

<industry name="healthcare">
Doubt: will they take my insurance, can I be seen, will they listen. Reviews are lost over the
practice, not the medicine: staff, listening, waits, billing. Proof: insurance accepted, clinician
credentials, reviews on the platforms patients use. Law: identifiable patient testimonials need
written authorisation (US HIPAA); "specialist" is restricted in dentistry; providers post the
good-faith-estimate notice (US No Surprises Act). Weight: treatments, clinicians, insurance and
fees, new patients, booking. Real team and premises; stock clinical scenes read as generic.
</industry>

<industry name="software">
Doubt: will it work for my case, what will it cost, who else uses it. Proof: the real product,
published pricing, integrations, security posture, case studies naming the customer, problem and
result; ratings on the software review platforms. Customer logos aid recognition; case studies are
the evidence. Weight: pricing, product, docs, security.
</industry>

<industry name="retail">
Doubt: is this the right item, what's the delivered total, can I send it back. Proof: product
images, specs, verified reviews with photographs, delivery cost and date, returns on the product
page. Mistakes: costs revealed at checkout, forced accounts.
</industry>

<industry name="nonprofit">
Doubt: what exactly do you do, and where does my money go. Few sites say; those that don't read as
inefficient or hiding it. Proof: use of funds, impact figures, downloadable financials, a
verifiable charity rating or seal. Several audiences: donors, volunteers, people seeking help.
Real stories used with consent.
</industry>

<sitemaps>
The pages a site of each kind usually needs, most necessary first. The minimum is
the first four or five. Add a page only when `public/` and `brand/` hold the facts for it. An empty
team, blog, FAQ or events page costs more trust than a missing one. `type` or `type/variant` as
it goes in the page's brief.

  trade, home service   homepage · service/index (when more than a few services) · service per
                        service · reviews · contact · about · work/index (with real job photographs) ·
                        faq · location (only per area with local proof) · pricing or financing · legal
  professional service  homepage · service per practice area · team + team/person · about · work
                        (case studies or representative matters) · contact · pricing ("how we
                        charge") · article/index · careers · legal
  clinic, dental        homepage · service per treatment · team + team/person · contact (booking,
                        new patients) · pricing (insurance and fees) · location per office · reviews ·
                        faq · legal (privacy notices)
  restaurant, café, bar homepage · menu (one per service, or one with sections) · contact (hours,
                        reservations) · about · events (private dining, what's on) · location per
                        site · work/index as a gallery only with real photographs · careers
  retail, ecommerce     homepage · product/index per category · product · about · contact ·
                        faq · legal (shipping, returns, privacy, terms) · location/index for stores
  software              homepage · product or service per feature or use case · pricing · work
                        (customer stories) · docs · article/index · about · careers · contact · legal
  hospitality, venue    homepage · product per room or space (a room is sold like a product: photographs,
                        what it holds, price, book) · product/index (all rooms) · events (private hire) ·
                        pricing (packages) · work/index (the spaces photographed) · contact · faq · about ·
                        location (getting here)
  nonprofit             homepage · about (mission, where the money goes) · service per programme ·
                        landing (donate) · events · team · article/index (stories) · contact · careers

A landing page sits outside the nav, one per campaign, whatever the kind of site.
</sitemaps>
